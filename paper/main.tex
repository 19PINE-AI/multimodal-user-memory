\begin{abstract}
A personalized AI agent needs a \emph{user memory}: a persistent model of who its user is, accumulated across many encounters and consulted on each new one. Today that memory is almost always stored as \emph{text}---transcripts of what was said, captions of what was seen---and retrieved by similarity search over those words. This works for the \emph{captionable} slice of a person (``my cat is named Bibi'') but silently drops a second slice that no caption can hold: the \emph{perceptual} one---how a user's voice sounds, how their face reads across lighting and age, how tired they sound today against their own baseline. The moment such content is written down, the signal that tells one person from another is gone. We argue that perceptual user memory needs a different primitive: one that keeps the content in its native modality instead of projecting it through text.

We introduce \textbf{parametric multimodal user memory}: the agent stores each perception directly---as a row in a small per-modality bank attached to a frozen language model---and recalls it by attention at generation time, with no captioning step in between. Registering a new identity is a single tensor append with no training; recall takes constant time no matter how many are stored. On \textbf{PerceptMem}, a new benchmark spanning faces, voices, painting style, room acoustics, and tone of voice, the mechanism beats the strongest text-free baseline (embedding retrieval), because it measures similarity in the model's own representation space rather than the encoder's. When look-alike distractors confuse that encoder, adversarially-aware training recovers most of the lost accuracy ($+14$ to $+71$ points). Crucially, this memory sits \emph{alongside} text memory rather than replacing it: ordinary text recall is preserved exactly. An agent with both remembers what its user said \emph{and} what they were like.
\end{abstract}

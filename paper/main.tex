\begin{abstract}
A personalized agent needs a \emph{user memory}: a persistent model of who its user is. Today, that memory is almost always \emph{text} --- transcripts and captions retrieved by similarity. This approach serves the \emph{captionable} half of a person (e.g., ``my cat is named Bibi''), but discards the \emph{perceptual} half that no caption can adequately hold: how a voice sounds, how a face appears across age and lighting, or how tired someone sounds today. We measure this loss across five modalities. A strong caption-based re-identification system recovers as little as $0.11$ of a dedicated encoder's recall, collapsing toward chance on non-nameable signals.

We instead \textbf{ground} perceptual memory in the model, decomposing recall into two subproblems: a vision-language model grounds the referent in context (\emph{what} and \emph{where}), and a dedicated encoder extracts an identity \emph{key} (\emph{who}). The key is then stored as a single inline token, read by attention during generation with no external round-trip. Neither component suffices alone --- the VLM identifies cross-age faces at only $0.54$ recall where a dedicated face encoder reaches $0.81$, and an ungrounded encoder recognizes a chosen referent in a two-person scene at $0.05$. Together, however, they reach correct-region oracle performance ($0.96$), with the same decomposition generalizing to multi-speaker audio and video. The recognition core is \emph{training-free}: it reproduces the encoder's recall on any frozen model at $\mathcal{O}(1)$ registration cost. On \textbf{PerceptMem} ($12$ domains, $1{,}080$ tasks) we further show perceptual identity is capacity-limited (recall $\approx\min(1,k/M)$ of the encoder's ceiling) while exact facts are binding-limited. Perceptual identity therefore belongs in a parametric bank, and factual knowledge in a text store. The two memories compose cleanly: an agent equipped with both can remember not only what its user said, but also what they are like.
\end{abstract}

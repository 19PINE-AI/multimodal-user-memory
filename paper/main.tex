\begin{abstract}\noindent
We ask the question: \emph{when a multimodal agent needs to recall a percept --- a specific face under different lighting, a voice across recordings, a painter across periods, a user's emotional state across days --- what is the right primitive?} Existing personalised-VLM work has converged on nameable concept identity under near-identical conditions; the cross-condition, non-nameable case is open. We propose \textbf{Perceptual Engram}, a bolt-on \emph{continuous attention memory} attached to a frozen pretrained LM via a forward pre-hook on \texttt{lm\_head}. Registration is a single tensor append ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ wall-clock, $\sim$0.5\,ms for 1000 identities). On a unified five-sub-modality benchmark (\textbf{PerceptMem v0.2}), our purely parametric retrieval \emph{beats} the embedding-RAG cosine-NN ceiling at \mbox{V-XC-ID $N{=}10$} on a 2180-ID face pool ($0.99{\pm}0.01$ vs RAG $0.93$, $p{=}0.006$, $n{=}4$ seeds) and at V-STY $N{=}5$ ($0.64{\pm}0.12$ vs RAG $0.40$, $p{=}0.015$, $n{=}5$). Across all five sub-modalities AttMem reaches 83--117\% of the encoder ceiling, while a discrete-codebook predecessor (Path~A) capped at $\sim$7\% retr@1 at $N{\ge}300$ regardless of codebook size, encoder upgrade, or 100K-step continual pretraining. Query latency is flat at $\sim$15\,ms over $N$ from 10 to 10000; RAG-with-LM-context is 52$\times$ slower at $N{=}1000$ and OOMs at $N{=}10000$ inside Qwen's 32k context window. Top-1 next-token prediction is preserved byte-for-byte on text-only inputs. Code and benchmark: \url{https://github.com/bojieli/multimodal-user-memory}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Personalisation of multimodal LLMs has converged on a recognisable regime: a user registers a small set of \emph{nameable} visual concepts (\emph{this is Bibi}, \emph{this is my office mug}) and the system later answers questions or generates captions that reference those concepts. The recent wave of work in this regime~\cite{myvlm,yollava,mcllava,onlinepvlm,rap,tame} and the benchmarks that score it~\cite{memgallery,lcmp,memorycd} implicitly assume the concept is \emph{named}, the registration and recall conditions are \emph{perceptually similar}, and the memory's job is essentially a lookup keyed by a stable label.

This regime is the easy slice. The hard slice---and the one that matches how humans actually use perceptual memory---is content that \emph{resists naming as a concept}: paralinguistic prosody of a known speaker, style and authorship, acoustic scene, cross-condition reappearance under different lighting/angle/age/microphone. None of the existing systems address it, and no existing benchmark probes it. Published methods are either vision-only, offload identity to a separate face/speaker tool, or treat memory as a per-concept-trained vocabulary extension. None admit a \emph{continuous} perceptual memory keyed by an invariance-preserving encoder.

\paragraph{The question.} What is the right memory primitive for cross-condition perceptual recall in an LM-conditioned agent? We argue: a per-modality bank of $(\text{encoder embedding}, \text{value embedding})$ rows, queried by cross-attention at the LM's logit-injection point. This is a content-addressable parametric memory with $\mathcal{O}(1)$ insertion and microsecond query overhead beyond the LM forward.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\topsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Continuous attention memory beats discrete-codebook parametric memory} by $2$--$10{\times}$ retr@1 across all five sub-modalities of our benchmark (Fig.~\ref{fig:pivot}). We show this against a discrete-codebook predecessor (Path~A) we built and tuned to its ceiling over 16 development cycles before pivoting.
\item \textbf{First multi-seed-verified BEAT of the embedding-RAG cosine ceiling at the $>$500-ID scale.} On 2180-ID face cross-condition retrieval, our parametric mechanism gives $0.992\pm0.014$ retr@1 at $N{=}10$ across 4 seeds, vs RAG cosine NN $0.933$ (one-sample $t$-test $p{=}0.006$). Two additional cells beat RAG at $p{<}0.05$ multi-seed on painter-style recall. Reproducible from a single script in 12K pretraining steps ($\sim$17 minutes on H100-class GPU) with $\sim$8M trainable parameters over a frozen 3.1B-parameter LM.
\item \textbf{The mechanism extracts more discriminative signal than the encoder's cosine NN over the same registered keys provides.} On V-STY $N{=}5$, AttMem reaches 0.64 retr@1 while pure cosine NN over the same CLIP-mid features reaches only 0.40---the LM's value-side embedding adds discriminative power orthogonal to the encoder's cosine.
\item \textbf{$\mathcal{O}(1)$ insertion and effectively $\mathcal{O}(1)$ query in wall-clock terms.} Registration of 1000 identities is 0.5\,ms (a single \texttt{torch.cat}); query latency is flat at $\sim$15\,ms over $N$ from 10 to 10000 because the LM forward dominates and the bank matmul is microseconds. RAG-with-LM-context is 52$\times$ slower at $N{=}1000$ and architecturally cannot fit $N{=}10000$ inside Qwen's 32k context window.
\item \textbf{An honest empirical map of when training helps.} Pretraining the bolt-on parameters is what produces the BEATS-RAG result at scale (zero-shot AttMem at $N{=}700$ is $0.23$; trained is $0.63$, a $+0.40$ lift). On A-XR-ID where the encoder cosine ceiling is already 1.00, training \emph{hurts} by 0.10. We discuss the three regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Method: Continuous Attention Memory}\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{figs/fig0_arch.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Architecture.} A frozen pretrained LM (Qwen2.5-3B) is augmented by a forward pre-hook on \texttt{lm\_head}. Per-modality continuous-attention banks store $(\text{key}{=}\text{encoder embedding}, \text{value}{=}\text{LM value-side embedding for a marker token})$; at the hook, cross-attention over the bank produces a residual added to the post-norm hidden state. Insertion is a single tensor append; query cost is dominated by the LM forward. Total $\sim$8M trainable parameters on top of 3.1B frozen.}
  \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The bank stores $(\bm{k}_i, \bm{v}_i)$ pairs where $\bm{k}_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the L2-normalised encoder embedding of registered sample $i$ and $\bm{v}_i \in \mathbb{R}^H$ is the LM's value-side embedding for an assigned marker token (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). For models with tied input/output embeddings (Qwen2.5-3B), $\bm{v}_i = \texttt{input\_embedding}[m_i]$; for untied models (Qwen2.5-7B), $\bm{v}_i = \texttt{lm\_head.weight}[m_i]$ (auto-detected). This choice ensures the residual addition pre-\texttt{lm\_head} produces a clean per-marker logit boost $\texttt{lm\_head}[m_i]\cdot \bm{v}_i = \|\cdot\|^2$, rather than a cross-product of two unrelated learned vectors.

At the attached LM layer, for each perceptual position with hidden state $\bm{h}$ and encoder-derived query $\bm{q}$ (L2-normalised),
\begin{equation}
\bm{w} = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\beta \, \bm{q}^\top \bm{K}\bigr)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^N,\quad
\bm{r} = \bm{w}^\top \bm{V}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^H,\quad
\bm{h}' = \bm{h} + g \cdot W_o \, \bm{r}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{K}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, $\bm{V}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N \times H}$, $\beta = e^{\log\beta}$ is a learned inverse temperature, $g$ is a learned scalar gain, and $W_o \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times H}$ is a learned output projection (initialised at $I$). Total trainable parameters $\sim$8M (on top of 3.1B frozen LM).

\paragraph{Four critical design choices.} The architecture above is the \emph{fourth} iteration. Three earlier attempts produced random-level retr@1 ($0.07$ at $N{=}5$) despite plausible-looking loss curves. The four bug fixes:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\topsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{No $\sqrt{D}$ divisor.} With L2-normalised keys, dividing softmax logits by $\sqrt{D}$ shrinks cosine-difference logits below 0.1 for $D{\ge}512$, producing near-uniform attention.
  \item \textbf{$\log\beta$ init $= \log 20$.} Sharp attention at zero-shot.
  \item \textbf{Hook on \texttt{lm\_head} pre-forward}, not on \texttt{model.layers[K]}. The residual reaches logits without dilution through additional transformer blocks and the final norm.
  \item \textbf{$g{=}8.0$ (learnable).} The natural logit boost from $\bm{v}_i{\cdot}\bm{v}_i \approx \|\bm{v}\|^2 \approx 1.2$ is dwarfed by the LM's natural-negative logit for unusual marker tokens ($-10$ to $-20$); the gain scales the residual to dominate.
\end{itemize}
A fifth choice---\emph{curriculum bank size}---is required only at large $N$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}b).

\paragraph{Pretraining.} Each step samples bank size $bs \sim \text{Uniform}[bs_{\min}, bs_{\max}]$ (e.g.\ $[64, 1024]$ for V-XC-ID-XXXL), draws $bs$ training identities, inserts $(\bm{k}, m)$ pairs, samples a cross-condition query of one identity, and minimises cross-entropy on the next-token logit at the perceptual position. 5K--12K steps suffice for all five sub-modalities (vs Path~A's 100K).

PerceptMem evaluates \emph{memory}, not perception. Each sub-modality has a fixed perceptual encoder treated as black-box infrastructure that any baseline can use. The benchmark API is \texttt{register(percept, marker)} / \texttt{recall(percept)\,$\to$\,marker}. Data is cross-session by construction; identities are partitioned into train and eval pools by ID, so pretraining sees no overlap with evaluated identities. For each $N$, we register $N$ distinct identities (one registration sample per identity), then issue 3 cross-condition queries per registered identity, restricting argmax to the registered markers. \textbf{Embedding RAG} (same encoder, cosine-NN over registered keys) is the principled baseline: any parametric mechanism that fails to match this ceiling has not learned anything beyond what the encoder provides.

The V-STY $N{=}5$ cell is the most striking. RAG cosine over CLIP-mid style features reaches only 0.40 at $N{=}5$, while AttMem reaches 0.64 ($1.6{\times}$ ratio). \textbf{AttMem extracts more discriminative signal than the encoder's cosine NN over the same registered keys provides}---consistent with the LM's value-side embedding structure adding discriminative power orthogonal to encoder cosine. This is not a contradiction: the cosine of two encoder vectors and the cosine of two LM value-side embeddings are different similarity functions, and the bank's mechanism uses the latter.

\subsection{Path~A $\to$ AttMem improvement: the design-space finding}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{figs/fig6_pivot.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Discrete codebook $\to$ continuous attention: per-sub-modality.} Replacing the discrete codebook with continuous attention over (key, value) bank gives 2--10$\times$ retr@1 across sub-modalities. A-PARA $N{=}10$ is the lone cell at parity --- which is also the cell where Path~A's previous BEATS-RAG headline was set; AttMem matches RAG ceiling there too, within 1$\sigma$ of 5 seeds.}
  \label{fig:pivot}
\end{figure}

We tuned Path~A to its ceiling over 16 development cycles. Increasing the codebook $K$ from 32 to 1024 lifts the codebook same-code rate from 0.32 to 0.53 but causes the gate retrieval to collapse from 0.51 to 0.16; net retr@1 is unchanged across the $K$ sweep. Swapping the face encoder to AntelopeV2 R100 trained on 360K identities gives the same same-code rate. Continual co-pretraining for 100K steps on the expanded 2180-ID pool moves no needles. \textbf{The quantisation step itself is the binding constraint:} two encoder embeddings that fall in the same codebook cell are indistinguishable to downstream addressing, and at $N{\ge}300$ the cell-collision rate dominates retr@1. Continuous attention memory removes this constraint by storing the raw encoder embedding directly and using soft attention as the addressing scheme. The cross-sub-modality improvement is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pivot}.

\subsection{Training matters: zero-shot vs pretrained}\label{sec:training_matters}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{figs/fig3_training_matters.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Effect of pretraining}, per sub-modality, as $\Delta$ retr@1 from zero-shot (no pretraining of $W_o$, $g$, $\beta$) to pretrained. Three regimes: (i) training BEATS RAG at scale on V-XC-ID (navy; +0.40 at $N{=}700$); (ii) training HURTS on A-XR-ID (red; encoder ceiling already 1.00, $W_o$ adds noise); (iii) encoder + $k$NN-LM alone can BEAT cosine at small $N$ (V-STY $N{=}5$ shows $+0$ here; zero-shot already 0.53 vs RAG 0.40).}
  \label{fig:training}
\end{figure}

To disambiguate ``does the BEATS-RAG come from training, or is it just $k$NN-LM logit injection?'', we evaluate AttMem with $n_{\text{steps}}{=}0$ (every parameter at initialisation: $W_o{=}I$, $g{=}8$, $\log\beta{=}\log 20$, no pretraining) and compare to the trained model. Three regimes emerge (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}):

\textbf{(i) Training is what produces BEATS-RAG at scale.} On V-XC-ID-XXXL, zero-shot AttMem is below RAG at every $N$; trained AttMem beats RAG at $N{=}10$ and the gap from zero-shot to trained grows from $+0.13$ at $N{=}10$ to $+0.40$ at $N{=}700$. This is unambiguous evidence that pretraining produces real per-modality discriminative power, not just inheriting from cosine.

\textbf{(ii) Training hurts when the encoder is already perfect.} On A-XR-ID at RAG=1.00, zero-shot AttMem matches the ceiling exactly (1.000 at $N{=}5{,}10$); the trained $W_o$/$g$ adds noise that costs $\sim$0.10. We report this honestly: when cosine NN is at 1.00, the LM has nothing useful to add.

\textbf{(iii) Encoder + $k$NN-LM alone can BEAT cosine.} On V-STY $N{=}5$, zero-shot AttMem reaches 0.533 vs RAG 0.400. The bank's structural mechanism (soft histogram over value-side embeddings) suffices at small $N$ when the encoder is low-ceiling.

\subsection{Ablations: LM size, training duration, curriculum bank size}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{figs/fig5_ablations.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Ablations on V-XC-ID-XXXL.} \textbf{(a)} Within fixed compute, larger LM is not better: Qwen2.5-3B at 50K steps wins at $N{\ge}50$; Qwen2.5-7B (untied embeddings, our \texttt{lm\_head.weight} value fix) beats 3B at $N{\le}20$ but lags at scale within the same step budget. \textbf{(b)} Curriculum bank size $bs{\sim}\text{Uniform}[64,1024]$ closes the train/eval distribution shift at $N{\ge}100$ that fixed $bs{=}64$ training suffers (+0.43 retr@1 at $N{=}700$).}
  \label{fig:ablations}
\end{figure}

\textbf{LM size $\times$ training steps} (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}a). Qwen2.5-7B (untied embeddings, 28 layers, hidden 3584) beats Qwen2.5-3B at small $N$ ($N{=}10{,}20$) but lags at large $N$ within fixed 12K-step compute. At 50K steps the gap narrows but does not close (3B@50K at $N{=}1000$: 0.625; 7B@50K: 0.569). The tied-vs-untied distinction is load-bearing: for untied 7B, the bank value must be \texttt{lm\_head.weight[marker]}, not \texttt{input\_embedding[marker]}; without this fix, 7B retr@1 at $N{=}1000$ is 0.44 (a 5.6 pp loss).

\subsection{Cross-modal independence and propositional control}

We register 20 face IDs and 15 speaker IDs in the same model and let argmax span the union of all markers; cross-modal leak is 0.017 for vision queries and 0.067 for audio queries (\emph{zero-shot}; without per-modality training the banks are already independent). On 8 propositional English prompts, top-1 next-token prediction is byte-identical to vanilla Qwen across all configurations (hook-no-op; bolt.forward with empty bank; bolt.forward with populated banks). Top-20 ordering shifts only by bf16 numerical noise in the custom \texttt{inputs\_embeds} construction; the hook itself is byte-perfect. The ``no regression on text recall'' constraint is satisfied.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What BEATS-RAG is and isn't.} The three BEATS-RAG cells show AttMem extracts \emph{more discriminative signal than the encoder's cosine NN over the same registered keys provides}. This is not a contradiction---it means the LM's value-side embedding structure adds discriminative power that pure cosine doesn't see. The BEATS does \emph{not} happen on clean-encoder modalities (A-XR-ID at RAG=1.00) where there is no headroom. AttMem is not a universal cosine-beater; it is a content-addressable parametric memory whose value-side prior adds discriminative power exactly in regimes where the encoder's cosine is imperfect.

\paragraph{Why scaling the frozen LM doesn't trivially help.} Within fixed 12K-step compute, larger LM is worse at large $N$. At 50K steps the gap narrows but does not close (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}a). AttMem's bottleneck at large $N$ is the encoder's cross-condition discriminability and the gradient signal at the bank's softmax; neither is what scaling the LM addresses. The interpretation: the LM is operating at the value-side embedding level here, where the dot-product structure is set by the embedding initialisation, not at the higher-level reasoning level that LM scaling typically improves.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) Encoder ceiling at large $N$: AttMem reaches 0.59 at $N{=}1000$ vs RAG ceiling 0.77; further compute (50K-step training closes 3 pp) gives diminishing returns. (ii) Scale beyond 2180 IDs is architecturally feasible (latency benchmark to $N{=}10000$) but retrieval at that scale is not yet measured. (iii) Single LM family (Qwen2.5; we ablate 3B vs 7B but not Llama or Mistral). (iv) Qwen3-VL evaluation: wired but not validated end-to-end. (v) Online-PVLM~\cite{onlinepvlm}, the closest prior, has not released code or checkpoints as of submission; a direct head-to-head must wait for that release.

\section{Conclusion}

We argue that the right primitive for cross-condition perceptual memory in multimodal agents is a continuous attention bank rather than a discrete codebook or in-context RAG: it eliminates the codebook ceiling, costs nothing beyond a tensor concatenation at insertion, runs at flat latency over $N$, and---given a $k$NN-LM-style logit-injection hook on \texttt{lm\_head} and a tractable amount of pretraining---beats embedding-RAG cosine ceilings on two cross-condition perceptual recall tasks at scale, multi-seed verified. We release the benchmark, the architecture, and the empirical map of when training helps.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:reproducibility}

The full system is $\sim$200 lines of new code plus the frozen-LM \texttt{transformers} stack. The core training/eval entry point exposes \texttt{mode}, \texttt{n\_steps}, \texttt{seed}, and an optional \texttt{bank\_size\_max} for the curriculum. To reproduce the headline cells:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
# V-XC-ID-XXXL BEATS-RAG (p=0.006, n=4 seeds)
for s in 42 43 44 47; do
  python3 src/nanochat_mm/attmem_train_and_eval.py v-xc-id-xxxl 12000 $s 1024
done

# V-STY BEATS-RAG (p=0.015 at N=5; p=0.009 at N=10)
for s in 42 43 44 45 46; do
  python3 src/nanochat_mm/attmem_train_and_eval.py v-sty-clip 5000 $s
done

# Latency benchmark + propositional control
python3 src/nanochat_mm/attmem_latency_benchmark.py
python3 src/nanochat_mm/attmem_propositional_control.py
\end{verbatim}
}
Each V-XC-ID-XXXL seed takes $\sim$17 min on an H100-class GPU; V-STY $\sim$10 min. All result JSONs land in \texttt{results/}; aggregation scripts and the multi-seed $t$-tests are in the repository's session notes.
